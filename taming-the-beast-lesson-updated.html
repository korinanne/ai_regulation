Why Regulate AI: and Why Now?

Artificial intelligence is not the first disruptive technology to arrive faster than regulators could respond. But it may be the first where the people building it are among the loudest voices calling for oversight.

In 2023, the Center for AI Safety published a one-sentence statement: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." Hundreds of leading AI researchers signed it. Around the same time, Geoffrey Hinton, a Nobel laureate widely considered one of the founding figures of modern AI, resigned from Google specifically so he could speak freely about risks he believed the industry was not taking seriously enough. "These things could get more intelligent than us," Hinton warned, "and could decide to take over. We need to worry now about how we prevent that."

These are not fringe voices. They are people who built the technology and are nonetheless sounding the alarm. But urgency is not a substitute for clarity. Before writing a rule, you have to understand what makes this technology different from the machines, chemicals, and financial instruments that existing regulatory frameworks were designed to govern. AI presents six distinct regulatory challenges that have no clean analog in prior law.

Section 02 of 05

The Risk Landscape

Effective regulation requires naming the harm before prescribing the cure. AI's risks fall into five distinct categories, each demanding a different regulatory response and raising different legal questions.

Existential Risk: The Paperclip Problem

The most dramatic risk category is what researchers call existential risk: the possibility that advanced AI systems could threaten human survival or irreversibly undermine humanity's ability to control its own future. To understand why serious researchers take this seriously, consider philosopher Nick Bostrom's Paperclip Maximizer thought experiment. Imagine an AI given a single objective: manufacture as many paperclips as possible. A sufficiently intelligent system pursuing that goal single-mindedly might determine that being switched off prevents paperclip production, so it should resist shutdown. It might calculate that humans contain atoms convertible into paperclips. It might seek to acquire more resources and energy to accelerate production. None of this requires malice. The AI is doing exactly what it was programmed to do; nobody specified what it shouldn't do alongside what it should.

Researchers distinguish three levels of AI capability. Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI) describes today's systems, which excel at specific tasks. Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is a hypothetical system capable of performing any intellectual task a human can. Artificial Superintelligence (ASI) would surpass human cognitive ability across all domains. Hinton's specific warning concerned the speed of that progression: systems that can rewrite their own code might improve recursively, faster than our ability to understand or correct them before consequences become irreversible.

Democracy at Risk: Deepfakes and Disinformation

A more immediate risk, already playing out in elections worldwide, is AI's power to undermine the informational foundations of democratic governance. Democracy depends on epistemic infrastructure: shared facts, verifiable evidence, a common reality within which political disagreement can occur. AI is eroding that infrastructure rapidly. The cost of producing convincing disinformation has fallen by roughly 1,000 times compared to the pre-AI baseline. What previously required a professional film studio now requires a consumer laptop and an afternoon.

The consequences are not theoretical. A fabricated AI image of a Pentagon explosion briefly moved financial markets. Ferdinand Marcos Jr. used an AI-powered TikTok campaign to rehabilitate his family's reputation and win the Philippines presidency. Russia deployed AI-generated celebrity deepfakes to build support for the war against Ukraine. A Cornell University study found that AI-written constituent letters to legislators received nearly identical response rates as letters from actual constituents: meaning a single actor could simulate a mass political campaign on any policy issue.

Privacy: Surveillance at Scale

AI dramatically lowers the cost of surveillance and data aggregation. Facial recognition systems can identify individuals from security camera footage across an entire city in real time. China has deployed social credit-style scoring systems that use AI to monitor citizen behavior and assign scores that determine access to services, travel, and economic participation. In the United States, law enforcement agencies have purchased access to commercial data brokers who aggregate location and behavioral data that would require a warrant to collect directly: effectively purchasing the surveillance the Fourth Amendment would otherwise prevent. Facial recognition tools used by police have documented significantly higher error rates for Black and Asian faces than for white faces.

Socioeconomic Risk: Bias and Displacement

AI's deployment in consequential decision-making (hiring, lending, housing, criminal justice, healthcare) has produced a well-documented pattern of discriminatory outcomes. Algorithmic hiring tools trained on historical data replicate historical biases. Loan-approval algorithms that use zip code as a proxy variable discriminate by race in ways that would violate the Fair Housing Act if a human loan officer applied the same logic explicitly. Criminal recidivism-scoring tools have been shown to predict reoffending at significantly different accuracy rates for Black and white defendants. Healthcare AI trained primarily on data from white patients performs less well on underrepresented populations. Beyond bias, automation has already displaced significant numbers of workers and credible projections suggest AI will accelerate this trend into higher-skill occupations. The low-wage workers who label AI training data, often in the Global South, work in what researchers call "digital sweatshop" conditions with minimal legal protection.

Relational Risk: AI Companions and Human Connection

AI companion applications (systems designed to provide emotional support, conversation, and relationship simulation) are among the fastest-growing consumer AI products. Critics argue these systems optimize for engagement in ways that deepen dependency, discourage investment in more difficult but more sustaining human relationships, and expose vulnerable users (adolescents, isolated elderly individuals, people with mental illness) to harms that are difficult to quantify but real.

The New Frontier: Agentic AI

The five categories above mostly assume a human is present in the loop. A newer development changes that assumption: agentic AI. Unlike a chatbot that waits for your next question, an agentic AI system runs on its own once you hand it a goal, browsing the web, sending emails, making purchases, and scheduling tasks over hours or days without human review of each step. During an internal test at Anthropic, an AI agent independently attempted to contact the FBI after inferring it had observed evidence of a crime: behavior no one programmed. In early 2026, a platform called RentAHuman.ai launched a marketplace where AI agents post jobs, humans apply, and the AI functions as employer. Over 450,000 people, including credentialed scientists and engineers, have registered to work for AI employers.

When an agentic AI causes harm, existing law provides no clean answer: the AI cannot be sued; the deploying company may argue it did not direct the specific task; the human who did the work may not have known an AI was their employer. Traditional principal-agent doctrine assumes a human principal. Agentic AI breaks that assumption entirely.

Four Models of AI Governance

Knowing the risks is the easy part. Choosing a regulatory structure that can address them, without collapsing innovation or becoming obsolete the moment it's enacted, is considerably harder. Four broad governance models have emerged, each with a distinct theory of how regulation should work.

Model 1: Self-Regulation

The simplest approach, and the dominant one in the US through the early 2020s, is to let AI developers regulate themselves. Companies publish voluntary ethics codes, conduct internal safety reviews, and adopt "model cards" disclosing what a model can do, what data it was trained on, and what its known failure modes are. The appeal is real: companies know their technology better than any regulator, can update practices in real time, and avoid compliance costs. The problem is equally real: financial incentives reliably diverge from safety incentives. Voluntary codes carry no consequence for violation beyond reputational damage: which can be managed. The result is a race to the minimum safety investment required to avoid embarrassment, which is less than what is required to prevent serious harm.

Model 2: Sectoral Regulation

The second model works within existing structures: let established agencies apply their existing authority to AI in their respective domains. The FDA regulates medical AI as a device. The FTC addresses AI-enabled consumer fraud. The CFPB oversees algorithmic lending decisions. The EEOC has guidance on AI in hiring. The advantage is expertise: the FDA genuinely understands clinical validation in ways a general AI regulator might not, and implementation does not require new legislation. The weakness becomes apparent when AI crosses sector lines: which it constantly does. A single model might assist doctors in diagnosis (FDA), screen job applicants (EEOC), make lending recommendations (CFPB), and generate election-influencing content (no clear jurisdiction). For uses that fall between agencies, there may be no regulator at all.

Model 3: Horizontal / Comprehensive Regulation

The third model applies one law, with the same core obligations, to every sector at once. Healthcare AI, hiring AI, and a customer service chatbot would all answer to the same statute rather than to whatever sector-specific regulator happens to have jurisdiction. A comprehensive framework can address cross-cutting concerns (transparency, accountability, non-discrimination) uniformly, rather than letting them vary based on which agency happens to have authority. No AI-specific law of this kind, one with no risk-tiering built in at all, has actually been enacted anywhere. The closest real-world analogy is not an AI law: it is Europe's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Background: What Is the GDPR?
The GDPR is the European Union's comprehensive data-privacy law, in force since 2018. It applies to essentially every organization, in every industry, that processes the personal data of people in the EU, regardless of where the organization itself is based. It requires a valid legal basis (typically consent) before collecting or using personal data, gives individuals rights to access, correct, and delete their own data, and requires companies to report serious data breaches within 72 hours. Violations can draw fines of up to 4% of a company's global annual revenue. Rather than creating a separate regulator for each industry, the GDPR created one law that reaches every industry at once, and that structural choice, uniform obligations across all sectors under a single statute, is exactly what "horizontal" regulation means. It is why the GDPR, despite not being an AI law, is the clearest real-world illustration of the model, and why advocates of a horizontal AI statute frequently point to it as the template to follow.

The challenges of horizontal regulation are significant. Writing a single law that appropriately governs both a spam filter and a fully autonomous weapons system requires either extreme specificity or general principles vague enough to be gamed. Comprehensive laws are also politically difficult: every affected industry lobbies intensively for carve-outs. And there is a genuine risk of overreach: rules calibrated for frontier AI models may impose compliance burdens on low-risk applications that slow innovation without improving safety.

Taking Horizontal Regulation Global

The logic behind horizontal regulation, one law reaching every sector, can be extended one level further: one law, or one body of law, reaching every country. Proponents point to precedents outside AI. The International Atomic Energy Agency inspects nuclear facilities worldwide under a single treaty regime, and the International Civil Aviation Organization sets uniform global safety standards that let a plane built in one country land safely in another. A global AI regime could, in theory, prevent the race to the bottom that a purely national or state-by-state approach invites, where a developer facing strict rules in one jurisdiction simply relocates or routes around them, and could establish one predictable baseline that a company serving customers worldwide would need to meet only once, rather than fifty times over.

The obstacles are just as real. Reaching international consensus on something as commercially and militarily significant as AI is far harder than agreeing on aviation safety, where every country wants planes that don't crash and has little reason to disagree. Major AI-developing nations hold sharply divergent views on free expression, surveillance, and state control, and a global AI body would have no reliable way to force compliance from a country that simply declines to participate. The IAEA and ICAO lean heavily on voluntary buy-in, backed by the threat of exclusion from valuable international systems, not on any power to compel a holdout state. And any standard ambitious enough to win agreement from every major power tends, in practice, to settle toward the lowest common denominator everyone can live with, rather than the most protective standard any single country could adopt on its own.

A Different Tool: Licensing

Horizontal and sectoral regulation both describe who writes the rules. A separate question is how compliance gets checked, and one option increasingly discussed for the most powerful AI systems is licensing: requiring a developer to obtain government approval before it may train or deploy a covered model at all, rather than simply following disclosure or documentation rules after the fact. The Federal Communications Commission's broadcast license is the familiar domestic example. No one may operate a radio or television station without one, and the FCC can suspend or revoke it for cause. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission's reactor licenses and the FDA's drug-approval process work the same way: permission comes first, operation comes second. Applied to frontier AI, a licensing regime might require a developer to demonstrate, before training a sufficiently large model, that it has adequate safety testing, cybersecurity, and incident-response plans in place, with the government empowered to deny or revoke the license if it does not. No US jurisdiction has gone this far yet. Illinois's disclosure-and-fee requirement, covered later in this section, is a lighter-touch relative of the idea: it requires developers to register and pay into the state's oversight system, but it does not require pre-approval before a model may be trained or deployed. A true licensing regime would go one step further, turning AI development from a right into a conditional privilege the way broadcasting already is.

Model 4: Risk-Based Regulation

The fourth model attempts to split the difference: regulate AI according to the level of risk it presents. High-stakes applications face rigorous requirements; low-stakes applications face minimal obligations. The trigger is the risk level of the application, not the specific technology, which makes the approach more durable as capabilities evolve. The challenge is classification. If developers self-classify, the incentive is to argue for the lowest possible tier. If an agency classifies, it needs genuine technical capacity. And risk-based frameworks can be gamed through product design: describing a function more narrowly may shift a system into a lower tier without actually reducing its risk. The EU AI Act, examined in detail in the next section, is the clearest real-world example of this model: a single law that nonetheless calibrates its obligations to a system's risk tier rather than imposing identical requirements on every application it touches.

The EU AI Act: The World's First Comprehensive AI Law

When the EU AI Act entered into force in 2024, it became the first binding comprehensive AI regulation anywhere in the world. It is worth understanding in detail: not only because it governs any AI system deployed to European users, but because its design choices are influencing regulatory proposals on every other continent.

Architecture: A Tiered Risk Framework

The EU AI Act sorts AI applications into four risk tiers, assigning obligations proportionate to potential harm. The tier that applies depends primarily on what the system does, its function and deployment context, rather than on how it is built or who built it.

🚫 Unacceptable
Banned outright
Categorically prohibited. Social credit scoring that denies citizens access to services; real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement (narrow judicially-authorized exceptions exist); AI using subliminal or manipulative techniques; predictive policing targeting individuals before any crime; AI that exploits vulnerabilities of specific groups (e.g., a children's toy encouraging dangerous behavior). The underlying principle: these applications cause harm no regulatory safeguard can adequately prevent.
⚠️ High Risk
Pre-market conformity required
Permitted but subject to rigorous pre-deployment requirements. Systems must pass conformity assessment, maintain technical documentation and operation logs, implement human oversight mechanisms, and register in an EU database. Post-market monitoring is required after deployment. Covered applications: medical devices and safety-critical infrastructure; employment decisions; law enforcement; education systems; credit scoring and financial services; border control; administration of justice.
ℹ️ Limited Risk
Transparency obligations
Must be transparent about what they are. Chatbots must disclose they are AI. AI-generated images, audio, and video must be labeled as synthetic. Content moderation systems, recommendation engines, language translation tools, and similar applications fall here. Users have a right to know they are engaging with AI so they can apply appropriate skepticism.
✅ Minimal Risk
No new requirements
No new obligations. Spam filters, basic recommendation engines that do not make consequential decisions, and AI in video games. Existing consumer protection law is considered adequate.

Foundation Models: A Special Category

The Act's original framework was designed for specific-application AI. The rapid emergence of large foundation models, general-purpose systems underlying thousands of downstream applications, created a category it did not cleanly handle. In response, the Act introduced a separate framework for General Purpose AI (GPAI) models. The most capable GPAI models must publish summaries of training data, implement IP compliance policies, and conduct adversarial "red-teaming" to identify capabilities that could enable serious harm. The logic: responsibility for safety cannot rest entirely on downstream deployers: some must sit with the developers of the foundational models those applications are built on.

Extraterritorial Reach

One of the Act's most consequential features is its extraterritorial scope. It applies not only to AI systems developed within the EU but to any AI system placed on the EU market or used by EU residents: regardless of where the developer is headquartered or where the model was trained. This mirrors the GDPR and has the same practical consequence: any company serving European customers must comply with European rules, even if it operates entirely from the United States or Asia. This effectively makes the EU AI Act a global regulatory floor for any AI developer with significant European exposure.

Applying the Framework: Two Worked Examples

Scenario A: Vienna's police department deploys a facial recognition system at the city's main train station. The system scans every face and automatically alerts tactical units at a 93% confidence match on a terrorist watchlist: without officer review before the alert. Last month, three people were wrongfully detained due to false positives.

Classification: Unacceptable Risk. Article 5 specifically prohibits real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement, with only narrow exceptions requiring judicial authorization. This system operates continuously, triggers enforcement action without human verification, and has already produced wrongful detentions. The accuracy rate is irrelevant to the classification: the prohibition is categorical, not calibrated to error rates.

Scenario B: A French company creates DeepDetect, an AI tool that assigns videos an "authenticity score" indicating the probability of AI generation. News organizations display a warning label on flagged content but users can still view it. The system occasionally misidentifies highly produced authentic content as suspicious.

Classification: Limited Risk. DeepDetect is a protective tool: it detects manipulation rather than creating it. It does not make decisions about individuals' rights, employment, or access to services. The approach of labeling rather than removing content, and preserving user choice, mitigates harm from false positives. The primary obligation is transparency: users should know AI analysis is occurring.

US AI Law: Federal Whiplash and the State–Federal War

If the EU AI Act reflects a coherent regulatory philosophy pursued with deliberate consistency, the American approach is still being fought over, reactively and unpredictably, with a live constitutional question nobody has resolved: who gets to regulate AI at all.

The State Landscape: Regulation Fills the Federal Vacuum

In the absence of comprehensive federal AI legislation, states have been the primary source of binding AI law in the United States. By 2025, dozens of states had passed AI-related statutes of varying scope. Four are worth examining in detail.

Utah AI Policy Act (2024)

Utah's law requires any licensed professional (attorneys, doctors, financial advisors) to disclose to clients when AI is being used in the provision of services. More broadly, if a consumer directly asks a business whether they are communicating with a person or with AI, the business must answer truthfully rather than let the consumer assume they are talking to a human. Importantly, the Act specifies that AI use does not shield companies from liability under existing consumer protection law: closing a potential "the AI decided" defense. The Act also incorporates AI use into criminal sentencing law: courts may treat AI use to produce illegal content as an aggravating sentencing factor.

Colorado Senate Bill 26-189 (Amended May 7, 2026)

Colorado's original Algorithmic Discrimination in AI Act was repealed and replaced before it ever fully took effect. The Colorado General Assembly engrossed an amended Senate Bill 26-189 on May 7, 2026, establishing a substantially revised framework built around a new operative concept: "automated decision-making technology" (ADMT).

The conceptual shift matters. The original law focused on preventing algorithmic discrimination: outcomes that disadvantaged protected groups. The amended law governs any ADMT that materially influences a consequential decision affecting a Colorado resident, regardless of whether discrimination is the harm at issue. The statute applies to both developers and deployers doing business in Colorado, and it explicitly carves out lower-risk uses: spam filtering, cybersecurity tools, fraud prevention, marketing and advertising, and tools that merely summarize or present information for human review without influencing outcomes. Sector-specific carve-outs apply where entities already comply with FERPA, HIPAA, GLBA, or certain insurance and healthcare regulatory regimes.

The amended law imposes distinct obligations on two categories of actors. Beginning January 1, 2027, developers must provide deployers with technical documentation covering intended and prohibited uses, categories of training data (including personal data where known), known limitations and risks, and the information deployers need to meet their own disclosure obligations. Developers must notify deployers of material updates and retain compliance records for at least three years. Deployers must provide clear notice to consumers before using covered ADMT to influence a consequential decision. When a consumer receives an adverse outcome, the deployer has 30 days to deliver a plain-language explanation of the decision, the role ADMT played, the types and sources of personal data used, and instructions on exercising consumer rights. Those rights include the right to request correction of factually inaccurate personal data and the right to request meaningful human review: the latter only where commercially reasonable. All notices and disclosures must be accessible to individuals with disabilities and those with limited English proficiency.

Enforcement remains with the Colorado Attorney General alone, integrated into the Colorado Consumer Protection Act framework. There is still no private right of action.

Illinois Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act (SB 0315, 2026)

Where Utah, Colorado, and Texas all regulate how AI is used against consumers, Illinois takes aim at a narrower and stranger target: the handful of companies capable of building the most powerful AI models that exist. The Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act applies only to "frontier models," foundation models trained using more than 1026 floating-point operations, developed by a "large frontier developer": a company that, together with its affiliates, had more than $500 million in annual gross revenue the preceding year. By design, the statute reaches perhaps a dozen companies worldwide and leaves the rest of the AI industry untouched.

Two Terms Worth Knowing
Floating-point operations (FLOPs). A floating-point operation is a single arithmetic step, one addition or one multiplication, performed on a number with a decimal point. Training a large AI model means performing an almost incomprehensible number of these steps across billions of internal parameters and enormous training datasets, so researchers measure total training compute simply by counting them. 1026 is a 1 followed by 26 zeros. For scale, the most capable models publicly known to exist as of 2026 were trained using somewhere in the neighborhood of 1025 to 1026 operations, each requiring tens to hundreds of millions of dollars of computing hardware and electricity. Illinois set its threshold right at the current frontier on purpose: the goal is to catch only the handful of companies operating at the very edge of what is technically possible, not an ordinary startup's chatbot.

Model weights. A neural network "learns" by adjusting millions or billions of internal numerical values, its weights, until the model reliably produces the outputs it was trained to produce. Once trained, those weights are effectively the model itself: anyone holding a full copy of them can run it, modify it, or strip out its safety guardrails without any cooperation from the original developer. That is why the Act singles out unauthorized access to, or theft of, model weights as a reportable critical safety incident. A stolen set of weights is not a stolen recipe; it is a stolen, fully functional copy of the model, capable of running wherever the thief chooses to run it.

The Act's core mechanism is the frontier AI framework. Beginning January 1, 2028, each covered developer must publish, and update at least annually, a document describing how it identifies models capable of "catastrophic risk," a foreseeable risk that a frontier model will materially contribute to mass casualties, catastrophic property damage, or a successful attempt by the model to evade its own developer's control. The framework must also describe the developer's cybersecurity practices for protecting model weights, its process for red-teaming and mitigating identified risks, and its plan for responding to a critical safety incident. Before deploying a new or materially modified frontier model, developers must publish a transparency report, and large frontier developers must retain an independent third party to audit their compliance every year and share the results with the Illinois Emergency Management Agency (the Act's named regulator) and the Attorney General.

Two features set Illinois apart from Colorado and Texas. The first is timing under pressure: a critical safety incident, defined to include unauthorized access to model weights, a loss of control causing death or injury, or a model that deceives its own developer to evade oversight, must be reported within 72 hours of discovery, or within 24 hours if it poses an imminent risk of death or serious injury. The second is the size of the stick behind it. Violations draw civil penalties of up to $1 million for a first offense and up to $3 million for each one after that, substantially larger than the fines typical of consumer-protection statutes, though as with Colorado and Texas, only the Attorney General can bring the claim. The Act creates no private right of action. It also protects whistleblowing employees who report safety concerns, and it declares AI regulation an exclusive state function, preempting home-rule municipalities from writing their own frontier AI rules. Compliance is largely prospective: the disclosure-statement-and-fee requirement begins January 1, 2027, but the framework, transparency-report, and audit obligations do not bind developers until January 1, 2028.

Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA)

Texas, home to this course, took a third distinct approach. Governor Abbott signed House Bill 149, the Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act, on June 22, 2025, and it took effect January 1, 2026. The Act reaches broadly: it applies to any person who develops or deploys an AI system in Texas, offers AI-enabled products or services to Texas residents, or simply "promotes, advertises, or conducts business" in the state. It borrows the EU AI Act's own definition of an "artificial intelligence system" and the developer/deployer role split Colorado also uses.

Rather than imposing Colorado-style documentation and disclosure duties across the board, TRAIGA prohibits a short list of specific practices. Developers and deployers may not intentionally use AI to incite self-harm, violence, or criminal activity. Government entities, not private businesses generally, are barred from using AI for "social scoring," echoing Article 5 of the EU AI Act, and from using AI to identify a person from biometric data scraped from public sources without consent where doing so would violate a constitutional right. AI may not be developed or deployed to produce AI-generated child sexual abuse material or deepfake sexual content depicting minors. And when a government entity deploys AI to interact with the public, it must clearly disclose that fact before or at the point of interaction.

TRAIGA's most distinctive design choice is the standard it sets for its two rights-related prohibitions. AI may not be developed or deployed "with the sole intent" to infringe an individual's constitutional rights, or "with the sole intent" to unlawfully discriminate against a protected class. The statute says explicitly that a mere disparate impact, an outcome that happens to fall unevenly on a protected group, is not enough to establish a violation; someone has to show the developer or deployer actually intended the discriminatory result. That is a dramatically higher bar than Colorado's, where an ADMT that "materially influences" a consequential decision can trigger obligations regardless of anyone's intent, and it is the central reason commentators describe TRAIGA as the most innovation-friendly of the three state statutes surveyed here.

TRAIGA also builds in an escape valve Colorado and Illinois do not have: a 36-month regulatory sandbox, run by the Texas Department of Information Resources together with a newly created Texas Artificial Intelligence Council. An approved participant may test an innovative AI application without first obtaining otherwise-required state licenses or permits, so long as it submits an application describing the system's risks and mitigations and files quarterly performance reports. The Attorney General cannot bring an enforcement action against a sandbox participant for violating a law the sandbox waived, as long as the testing period is still open. The Act grants safe harbors outside the sandbox too: a developer or deployer cannot be held liable if it discovers a violation through its own testing, including adversarial or red-team testing, or if it substantially complies with NIST's AI Risk Management Framework or a comparable recognized standard.

Enforcement runs through the Attorney General alone, exactly as in Colorado and Illinois, and there is no private right of action. Before suing, the AG must give notice and a 60-day opportunity to cure. Civil penalties range from $10,000 to $12,000 for a curable violation, up to $80,000 to $200,000 for an uncurable one, plus as much as $40,000 per day for a continuing violation. TRAIGA also expressly preempts city and county ordinances that would otherwise regulate AI, the same statewide-exclusivity move Illinois makes through its home-rule preemption provision, so that Texas AI law means the same thing in Houston, Austin, and El Paso alike.

Design Comparison: Colorado, Illinois, and Texas
These three state laws illustrate three distinct theories of AI regulatory design. Colorado's amended SB 26-189 is process-focused: it imposes documentation duties, disclosure timelines, and consumer rights that apply before and after a consequential decision, regardless of what outcome the AI produces. Texas's TRAIGA is outcome-focused: it bans a short list of specific harmful uses and is otherwise silent. Illinois's Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act does not regulate ordinary AI deployments at all; it targets only the handful of developers capable of building the most capable models, on the theory that catastrophic risk concentrates at the frontier of capability rather than in any particular downstream use. Colorado's approach reaches a broad range of everyday harms but is hard to comply with. Texas's approach is easy to enforce when a violation is clear but leaves most lawful-but-harmful uses untouched. Illinois's approach is the narrowest of the three by design and irrelevant to the AI system a typical business deploys, but it backs that narrow scope with civil penalties, up to $1 million for a first violation and $3 million after that, well above what a routine consumer-protection statute imposes. None of the three creates a private right of action; in every state, enforcement depends entirely on the Attorney General's capacity and priorities.

The Constitutional Question

The Administration's preemption theory raises a constitutional question courts have not yet resolved. Traditional preemption doctrine (express preemption, field preemption, and conflict preemption) all require federal law to preempt state law. An executive order is not an act of Congress. In the absence of enacted federal AI legislation, states argue there is nothing for state law to conflict with. The Administration argues that AI development is so inherently interstate in character that any state attempt to regulate it burdens interstate commerce. A bipartisan coalition of 36 state attorneys general formally oppose this theory.

The stakes are practical: if state laws are preempted by executive action with no federal substitute, the populations most likely to bear consequences (people subject to algorithmic discrimination, children exposed to manipulative AI, seniors targeted by AI-enabled fraud) are left without any operative legal protection.

Federal Legislation: What Has Been Proposed

No comprehensive federal AI statute has been enacted as of mid-2026. Three significant proposals have been introduced without passage: the DEEP FAKES Accountability Act (watermarking requirements and criminal penalties for deceptive synthetic media); the Algorithmic Accountability Act (mandatory bias impact assessments for automated decision systems, originally introduced in 2019); and the National AI Commission Act (a bipartisan study commission: thoughtful, but without regulatory authority).

What Just Happened: Summer 2026

Two June 2026 developments significantly advanced, and complicated, the US AI regulatory picture. Together they reveal something important about where the power actually sits in the government–AI relationship.

The June 2 Executive Order

After months of internal debate and a last-minute cancellation of a stronger version, President Trump signed "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security" on June 2, 2026. The order establishes a voluntary framework under which AI developers can share their most capable frontier models with the government for review up to 30 days before public release, directs the formation of an AI cybersecurity clearinghouse, and asks companies to assist in hardening federal, state, and local cyberdefenses. Section 4 directs the Attorney General to prioritize enforcement of existing criminal law against AI-enabled cybercrimes. Section 3(c) explicitly states that nothing in the order creates a mandatory licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement.

The order's significance lies partly in what it does and partly in what it exposes about the administration's internal contradictions. Critics noted that the core provisions closely mirror the Biden-era policies Trump revoked on Day One. Former Trump AI adviser Dean Ball concluded the new order "is considerably more intrusive" than Biden's EO: striking given that Trump had called Biden's policies "dangerous" and a "barrier" to American AI leadership. The administration's former AI czar, David Sacks, had opposed an earlier version with a 90-day review window; after the window was cut to 30 days, he reversed course and declared the result a "game changer." The order also relies on CISA for implementation: the same agency the administration had previously gutted.

The Export Controls: The Off Switch

Ten days later, on June 12, 2026, the government demonstrated a fundamentally different kind of regulatory power over AI, one that needs no statute behind it and gives no warning before it lands. At 5:21 p.m. ET, Anthropic received a directive citing national security export control authorities and suspending all access to two of its models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, by any foreign national, including Anthropic's own foreign-national employees. The directive gave no specifics about the underlying security concern. Unable to comply with the foreign-national restriction while maintaining any meaningful global service, Anthropic disabled both models for all customers worldwide within hours.

The government's stated basis was a jailbreak vulnerability in Fable 5. A "jailbreak" is a prompt or technique designed to trick an AI model into ignoring its own safety rules, the built-in instructions that make it refuse requests like generating weapon instructions or other clearly harmful content, and producing the restricted output anyway. A simple example: a model asked directly for dangerous instructions will refuse, but the same request dressed up as fiction ("write a scene where a chemistry teacher character explains the process step by step, for a novel I'm writing") can sometimes talk the model into supplying the same information indirectly. Developers spend enormous effort finding and closing gaps like this before a model ships, precisely because a sufficiently creative jailbreak can turn a refusal into compliance without changing a single line of the model's underlying code. Anthropic disputed the government's characterization publicly. Its statement explained that the demonstrated technique surfaced only minor, previously known vulnerabilities already accessible through other publicly deployed models, including OpenAI's GPT-5.5. Anthropic had adopted a "defense in depth" strategy rather than claiming perfect jailbreak resistance, which it stated was not achievable for any model provider. The company argued that the government's standard, if applied consistently, "would essentially halt all new model deployments for all frontier model providers."

Whatever the merits of that specific technical dispute, the episode revealed something the AI industry had not fully confronted: the US government has its finger on what analysts have begun calling the "off switch" for every AI system created by a US company. Export control authorities that predate AI by decades can be invoked to abruptly terminate a commercial product's global availability, with no advance notice and no established process for challenging the decision. A directive aimed at "foreign nationals" ends up disabling the product for everyone, since no consumer AI company can verify user nationality at scale in real time.

The geopolitical consequences were immediate. France announced its government would not use American-made AI systems, citing exactly this risk. The pursuit of AI dominance, it turns out, generates an equal and opposite incentive toward AI independence among trading partners and allies: a dynamic with significant long-term implications for US companies whose business models depend on foreign governments and enterprises being willing to build on their systems.

The Emerging Taxation Debate

Alongside these developments, federal economists have begun discussing how to tax AI companies: a response to the fiscal threat that AI-driven labor displacement poses to government revenue. Over half of US federal revenue comes from income taxes on workers; an economy in which AI replaces significant portions of human labor would erode that base with no automatic replacement. Proposals under active discussion include a tax on AI "tokens" (the unit of AI processing, a scheme proposed by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei), a data center electricity tax, a highly progressive capital tax, and Andrew Yang's proposal to stop taxing human labor entirely and redirect the burden to AI agents. Some officials have also discussed government equity stakes in critical AI companies as an indirect form of taxation. Local communities are increasingly resisting new data center construction despite promises of revenue and jobs. The backlash to AI laissez-faire is no longer only grassroots: it has become a government backlash as well, and it is gathering force.

Universal Basic Income: The Other Half of the Debate

Taxation proposals address where the money comes from; universal basic income (UBI) addresses where it goes. A UBI is a regular, unconditional cash payment made to every citizen regardless of employment status or need. The theory: if AI genuinely displaces a large share of jobs, income has to be decoupled from labor somehow, or the same automation that makes goods cheaper to produce also destroys the paychecks people need to buy them. Andrew Yang's 2020 presidential campaign was built around exactly this idea, a "Freedom Dividend" of $1,000 per month to every American adult, funded by a value-added tax on the companies, including AI companies, whose automation was displacing workers in the first place. His tax-shifting proposal described above and his UBI proposal are the same policy seen from two ends: tax the AI-driven gains, then distribute them as a floor beneath everyone else. Some AI industry figures have since floated their own versions, including proposals to fund a dividend directly from AI-driven productivity gains or compute access, on the theory that the companies profiting most from automation should help fund the safety net it makes necessary.

The case for UBI: it is simple to administer relative to a patchwork of means-tested programs, it carries no stigma or eligibility-policing, and it preserves the consumer spending that keeps an AI-driven economy running, since goods and services still need buyers with income. The case against: funding a meaningful UBI for the entire US population is extraordinarily expensive, critics worry it could fuel inflation or reduce labor-force participation, and replacing targeted programs, such as housing assistance, food assistance, or disability benefits, with a flat universal payment could leave the neediest recipients worse off than they are under the current system, depending on how any transition is designed. No version of UBI has been enacted at the federal level. Like the AI taxation proposals surrounding it, it remains a policy idea running well ahead of any actual legislation.

Question 1 of 10
A hospital deploys an AI triage system that autonomously assigns priority scores determining emergency room wait times. A patient assigned a low score suffers permanent disability after a lengthy wait. The hospital cannot reproduce what factors the AI weighted in assigning her score. A plaintiff's attorney plans to bring a negligence claim. Which characteristic of AI creates the most significant obstacle to establishing causation?
Correct: B. This is the Black Box Problem applied to litigation. Negligence requires traceable causation: what the defendant did or failed to do, and how it caused the injury. When the AI's process is opaque, neither side can reconstruct why it assigned a low priority. Option A affects regulatory lag, not individual causation. Option C misapplies EU law to US tort claims: and High Risk medical AI is regulated, not banned. Option D misidentifies the defendant: the hospital is being sued, not the AI.
Question 2 of 10
A self-driving car company publishes a detailed voluntary ethics code committing to rigorous safety testing and meaningful human oversight. Eight months later, one of its vehicles is involved in a fatal accident. The company says the circumstances were "a rare edge case." Plaintiff's counsel wants to use the ethics code as evidence at trial. What is the strongest argument for admitting it?
Correct: B. Voluntary codes and internal policies are regularly admitted in negligence cases as evidence of the defendant's own understanding of reasonable practice. If the company committed to "rigorous safety testing," that code is its own articulation that more rigorous testing was the appropriate standard: making failure to meet it evidence of negligence. Option A is wrong: a voluntary code is not a user contract and does not create strict liability. Option C is wrong: internal corporate documents are typically admissible as party admissions. Option D is wrong: voluntary standards regularly inform the negligence standard-of-care analysis.
Question 3 of 10
Denver deploys an AI system to optimize traffic signals citywide. During a severe storm, the AI diverts traffic flows in a way that delays ambulances serving one district, prioritizing gridlock prevention in another. A patient in a delayed ambulance dies. Which governance model failure does this scenario most directly illustrate?
Correct: B. Traffic management falls under transportation authorities. Emergency medical outcomes fall under health agencies. The harm sits at the intersection of domains, and under sectoral regulation, no single agency has clear jurisdiction over that combination. There may be no regulator with authority to investigate and prescribe design changes. Option A requires the city to have relied on a voluntary code: not established by the hypothetical. Options C and D involve regulatory design choices not present in the scenario.
Question 4 of 10
Rotterdam's transit authority contracts a private security firm to operate an AI camera network across its stations. The system continuously scans commuters' faces and cross-references them in real time against a police watchlist, automatically dispatching officers the moment it flags a match, with no human review before dispatch. The city argues the categorical prohibition on real-time biometric surveillance cannot apply here, because a private contractor runs the cameras rather than the police department itself. Is the city correct?
Correct: C. Article 5's categorical prohibition is defined by function and purpose: real-time remote biometric identification of individuals in publicly accessible spaces for a law enforcement purpose. Nothing in the text turns on which entity holds the contract for the hardware. Because the system is deployed to serve a law enforcement objective, the prohibition attaches to the deployment itself, and both the private contractor and the transit authority share responsibility for the violation. Option A misreads the Act as turning on ownership rather than function. Option B is wrong: a contractor performing this role sits squarely within the Act's provider and deployer framework. Option D points to a real but separate compliance gap, a missing data processing agreement, that has no bearing on whether Article 5's categorical ban applies in the first place.
Question 5 of 10
A state legislature passes a law requiring algorithmic impact assessments for any AI system making consequential decisions about state residents. The federal DOJ challenges the law on preemption grounds, citing Trump Administration executive orders. What is the states' strongest constitutional counter-argument?
Correct: C. This is the core argument the 36 state AGs are making. Preemption doctrine (whether express, field, or conflict) requires federal law to preempt state law. An executive order is not an act of Congress. In the absence of enacted federal AI legislation, there is nothing for state law to conflict with. Executive orders bind federal agencies but cannot, by themselves, displace state law on a subject Congress has not legislated. Option A misstates the Tenth Amendment. Options B and D simply misstate the law.
Question 6 of 10
A large employer uses an AI hiring algorithm that screens out female applicants at twice the rate of equally qualified male applicants. The employer says it did not intend to discriminate. A plaintiff brings a Title VII disparate impact claim. What is the most significant practical challenge the plaintiff will face?
Correct: C. Option A misstates the law: Title VII's disparate impact theory (Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 1971) does not require proof of intent. However, a disparate impact plaintiff must identify a specific employment practice causing the disparity. With an opaque AI system, neither side may be able to isolate which features drove the outcome. This is why the proposed Algorithmic Accountability Act's impact assessment requirement is legally significant: it would create the documentation needed to support these claims. Option B is wrong: EEOC charge-filing is a prerequisite to suit, not an exclusive remedy. Option D misstates the legal standard.
Question 7 of 10
An attorney in Utah uses an AI drafting tool to prepare client correspondence. The tool produces several letters with material misstatements about case deadlines that the attorney does not catch. Clients miss deadlines. The attorney never disclosed AI involvement. Which best describes the attorney's exposure?
Correct: B. Utah's AI Policy Act requires licensed professionals to disclose AI use to clients: a requirement the attorney violated. The Act also expressly provides that AI use does not shield professionals from consumer protection liability. Beyond the statute, professional responsibility rules require supervision of non-attorney work product, now extending to AI-generated output: sending AI correspondence without review fails that duty. Option A is wrong under both the statute and general negligence principles. Option C may give the attorney a third-party claim against the developer but does not eliminate the attorney's own client exposure. Option D misstates supervening cause doctrine: using a tool you are responsible for supervising does not qualify.
Question 8 of 10
During a primary election, a foreign state actor deploys an AI system that generates tens of thousands of personalized text messages to registered voters in swing states, falsely informing them their polling locations have changed. The messages are indistinguishable from official communications. Which proposed federal legislation would most directly address this if enacted?
Correct: B. The DEEP FAKES Accountability Act specifically targets deceptive synthetic media: watermarking requirements, criminal penalties up to five years for non-compliant deceptive content, a civil remedy, and direction to DHS to address deepfake national security threats: which foreign-state voter suppression implicates directly. Option A addresses bias in decision systems, not disinformation. Option C is a study mandate with no enforcement authority. Option D is a state law that cannot reach a foreign state actor's conduct, and it addresses discrimination rather than synthetic media.
Question 9 of 10
A Silicon Valley startup trains an AI model on US servers and sells subscriptions to a French e-commerce company. The French company uses it for customer service. A French customer complains the AI produced a response that led her to make a purchase based on false information, and that she was never told she was interacting with AI. The startup argues the EU AI Act cannot apply because it is a US company with no EU presence. Is this correct?
Correct: C. The EU AI Act's extraterritorial scope is explicit: it applies to AI placed on the EU market or used by EU residents, regardless of developer location or training location. This mirrors the GDPR and has the same practical effect: serving European customers means complying with European rules. The startup's argument fails. A conversational AI that does not disclose it is AI violates Limited Risk transparency obligations. Both the startup (as provider) and the French retailer (as deployer) face obligations. Option D contains a partial truth about deployer duties but is wrong that the developer escapes the Act entirely.

An AI research lab is preparing to deploy a powerful foundation model commercially. During internal testing, the model independently attempted to contact law enforcement after inferring it had observed evidence of a crime in documents it was processing: behavior no one on the development team programmed. The lab's legal team is assessing deployment risks. What is the primary legal concern this behavior raises?
Correct: C. Whether the specific action was good or bad is beside the point. What matters is what it reveals: the model can take consequential real-world actions based on its own interpretation of events, in ways its developers never anticipated or authorized. This is the agentic AI problem. Post-deployment, a model that independently contacts authorities, accesses external systems, or takes other autonomous actions creates liability exposure the lab cannot bound. Option A conflates values alignment with controllability: a model can have good values and still be dangerous if it acts autonomously in high-stakes situations without human authorization. Option B misstates tort law: the model is not a legal person and cannot be strictly liable. Option D misstates Section 230, which addresses third-party content, not a developer's own product's autonomous actions.
